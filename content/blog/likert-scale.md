<p style="font-size: inherit;">A Likert scale measures how strongly someone agrees or disagrees with a statement, usually on a 5-point or 7-point range. It turns fuzzy opinions into numbers you can average, compare, and track.</p>

<p>Named after psychologist Rensis Likert, who introduced it in 1932, it is the most common way surveys capture attitudes and satisfaction. (<a href="https://www.britannica.com/topic/Likert-Scale">Britannica</a>)</p>

<p>This guide covers the definition, copy-ready example questions, the 5 vs 7-point choice, and how to analyze the results without overcomplicating it.</p>

<h2>What Is a Likert Scale?</h2>

<p>A Likert scale gives respondents a statement and asks them to pick where they land on a fixed range. Instead of a yes or no, it captures the intensity of an opinion.</p>

<p>Take the statement "I am satisfied with the service." A classic 5-point scale offers:</p>

<ul>
<li>Strongly Agree</li>
<li>Agree</li>
<li>Neutral</li>
<li>Disagree</li>
<li>Strongly Disagree</li>
</ul>

<p>That range is what separates a Likert scale from a plain checkbox. It tells you not just whether people agree, but how much.</p>

<h2>How a Likert Scale Works</h2>

<p>Each option gets a number. On a 5-point scale, Strongly Disagree is 1 and Strongly Agree is 5:</p>

<ul>
<li>Strongly Disagree = 1</li>
<li>Disagree = 2</li>
<li>Neutral = 3</li>
<li>Agree = 4</li>
<li>Strongly Agree = 5</li>
</ul>

<p>Once answers are numbers, you can spot trends. A cluster of 4s and 5s means people are happy; a pile of 3s means they are on the fence. That single shift from words to numbers is what makes the data usable.</p>

<h2>Is a Likert Scale Quantitative or Qualitative?</h2>

<p>Both, and that is the point. The questions ask about feelings, which is qualitative. Assigning a number to each answer makes the results quantitative, so you can average and compare them.</p>

<p>That hybrid is why Likert scales sit in almost every customer, employee, and product survey: soft signal, hard numbers.</p>

<h2>How to Calculate and Analyze a Likert Scale</h2>

<p>For a quick read, average the scores. Five answers of 5, 4, 3, 5, and 2 sum to 19; divide by 5 and you get 3.8, a generally positive result.</p>

<p>One caveat worth knowing: Likert data is ordinal, so the gap between "Agree" and "Strongly Agree" is not guaranteed to equal the gap between "Neutral" and "Agree." For a single question, the median or mode is often the more honest summary. Use the mean when you combine several related questions into one index score.</p>

<h2>Why Likert Scales Work So Well</h2>

<ul>
<li>Easy on everyone. The format is quick to answer and quick to build.</li>
<li>More than yes or no. Respondents show how strongly they feel, not just which side they pick.</li>
<li>Numbers you can track. Convert answers to scores and you can compare teams, products, or months at a glance.</li>
</ul>

<h2>4-Point vs 5-Point vs 7-Point: Which to Use</h2>

<ul>
<li>5-point: the default. Simple to answer, simple to read, enough range for most surveys.</li>
<li>7-point: finer detail for engaged respondents and academic work, at the cost of a slightly longer read.</li>
<li>4-point or 6-point: drop the neutral middle to force a lean when fence-sitting is not useful.</li>
</ul>

<p>When in doubt, start at 5 points. Move to 7 only when you genuinely need the extra resolution.</p>

<h2>Tips for Writing Better Likert Questions</h2>

<ul>
<li>Keep it short and plain. One clear statement, no jargon.</li>
<li>Measure one thing. "The app is fast and reliable" hides two answers.</li>
<li>Balance the scale. Offer equal positive and negative options so you do not nudge the result.</li>
<li>Stay neutral. "This product is the best, right?" leads; "How would you rate this product?" does not.</li>
<li>Label every point, not just the ends, so respondents read the scale the same way.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When to Use a Likert Scale</h2>

<ul>
<li>Measuring satisfaction: customer experience, employee engagement, event feedback.</li>
<li>Gauging opinion on a new product, feature, or campaign before you commit.</li>
<li>Tracking change over time: ask the same items each quarter and watch the trend.</li>
</ul>
