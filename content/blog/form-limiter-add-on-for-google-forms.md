<p style="font-size: inherit;">If you're hunting for the form limiter add-on, there's something you should know first. Google Forms can now cap responses and close on a deadline on its own, for free.</p>

<p>The setting just isn't where anyone looks for it. It's behind the Publish button, not in Settings, which is why thousands of people still go shopping for an add-on every month.</p>

<blockquote><p>To find the setting, click the sliders icon beside <strong>Publish</strong>, then <strong>Set close date or response limit</strong>. You pick either a date or a number of responses, not both. And there's no setting anywhere that opens a form on a schedule.</p></blockquote>

<h2>The native setting, and where Google hides it</h2>

<p>Open your form and look at the top right. Next to the <strong>Publish</strong> button there's a small sliders icon. Click it.</p>

<ol>
<li>Click the sliders icon beside <strong>Publish</strong> to open <strong>Published options</strong>.</li>
<li>Click <strong>Set close date or response limit</strong>. Google still badges it <strong>New</strong>.</li>
</ol>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/gforms_published_options_set_close_date_c3bca22b2b.jpg" alt="Google Forms Published options dialog with a Set close date or response limit link marked New" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;max-width:900px;height:auto;border-radius:8px;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;margin:16px 0;" /></p>

<p>Nothing in Settings points you here. Settings has a "Limit to 1 response" toggle, but that caps each person at one entry, it doesn't cap the form. Two different jobs, and the naming does you no favours.</p>

<h2>How to close a form after a number of responses</h2>

<p>In the dialog that opens, pick <strong>After a number of responses</strong> and type your number.</p>

<ol>
<li>Choose <strong>After a number of responses</strong>.</li>
<li>Enter the cap, then <strong>Save</strong>.</li>
</ol>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/gforms_stop_after_number_of_responses_d1b5a19e66.jpg" alt="Google Forms Stop accepting responses dialog with After a number of responses selected showing a responses count field" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;max-width:900px;height:auto;border-radius:8px;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;margin:16px 0;" /></p>

<p>Google counts every submission and shuts the form at your number. Late arrivals get a message instead of a form, so you don't end up with entries you can't honour.</p>

<h2>How to close a form on a date instead</h2>

<p>Same dialog, pick <strong>On a date</strong> and set a date and time.</p>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/gforms_stop_accepting_on_a_date_a00cec6df3.jpg" alt="Google Forms Stop accepting responses dialog with On a date selected and a choose date and time link" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;max-width:900px;height:auto;border-radius:8px;border:1px solid #e5e7eb;margin:16px 0;" /></p>

<p>You can also rewrite what people see once it's shut. The default reads "This form is no longer accepting responses", and the pencil icon lets you replace it with something useful, like where to go instead.</p>

<h2>The catch: it's a choice, not a combination</h2>

<p>Look at those two screenshots again. <strong>On a date</strong> and <strong>After a number of responses</strong> are radio buttons. Picking one clears the other.</p>

<p>So you can run "close at 100 seats" or "close on Friday at 6pm", never both. For a limited-seat event with a registration deadline, that's exactly the combination you want and can't have.</p>

<p>There's a second gap. The dialog is titled <strong>Stop accepting responses</strong>, and stopping is all it does. There's no scheduled open, so a form that shouldn't go live until Monday morning needs you to be awake and clicking on Monday morning.</p>

<h2>So what is the form limiter add-on now?</h2>

<p>Here's where it gets confusing, and it's worth five minutes of your attention before you install anything.</p>

<p>The formLimiter people remember was written by Andrew Stillman for New Visions for Public Schools, a non-profit in New York. It was free and open source under the GPL, and it was everywhere in schools. The code is still on GitHub.</p>

<p>Its last commit was in <strong>August 2013</strong>.</p>

<p>The Form Limiter in the Marketplace today is a different product from a company called Skyfield. That listing is genuinely maintained, updated within the last few days at the time of writing, but it isn't the free school tool people are remembering.</p>

<p>Note the second column. Response limits and email notifications are <strong>separate</strong> purchases. Wanting a form that caps at 100 and emails you on every entry means two subscriptions, not one.</p>

<h2>When the add-on still earns its money</h2>

<p>It isn't a rip-off. It does things the native setting doesn't, and if you need one of these, paying is reasonable.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Recurring schedules.</strong> Open every Monday at 9 and close every Friday at 5, week after week, without touching it.</li>
<li><strong>Per-question limits.</strong> Cap individual answer choices, so "Saturday 2pm" disappears once twelve people have taken it. That's Choice Eliminator territory and the native setting does nothing like it.</li>
<li><strong>Staying inside Google.</strong> If your school or company won't approve another tool, an add-on on an existing form is the shorter conversation.</li>
</ul>

<p>If none of those describe you, the native setting almost certainly covers it, and you can stop reading here with $49 a year still in your pocket.</p>

<p>This is the gap that sends people looking for add-ons in the first place, and it's the one thing neither the native setting nor a single Skyfield subscription solves cheaply.</p>

<p>In <a href="https://formester.com/features/form-limiter/">Formester's form limiter</a> the controls are independent toggles rather than radio buttons, so they stack. Open the form, go to <strong>Settings</strong>, then <strong>Access and scheduling</strong>.</p>

<h2>Where teams hit this</h2>

<p>The combination problem is almost always about capacity plus time.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Limited-seat events.</strong> Fifty places and a Friday cut-off. Whichever comes first should close it. See our guide to <a href="https://formester.com/blog/how-to-use-google-forms-for-event-registration/">using Google Forms for event registration</a> for the wider setup.</li>
<li><strong>First-N promotions.</strong> The first 200 entries, and nothing after the campaign ends.</li>
<li><strong>Shift and slot sign-ups.</strong> A weekly window that opens and closes on schedule, with a cap per run.</li>
<li><strong>Applications.</strong> A hard deadline, plus a ceiling on how many you'll review.</li>
</ul>
