<p style="font-size: inherit;">Google Forms has no built-in equation editor. Type a fraction or a square root and you get "1/2" or "sqrt(2)", which looks wrong to a student staring at a math test.</p>

<p>The fix is to add the formula as a clean image. Three methods cover almost every case: the Equatio extension for daily use, CodeCogs for the odd equation, and Microsoft Office if you already live in Word or PowerPoint.</p>

<p>Below, each method in numbered steps, a quick comparison so you can pick fast, and a simpler route if you would rather skip add-ons entirely.</p>

<h2>Can Google Forms Handle Math?</h2>

<p>Short answer: not on its own. Google Forms cannot render equations, run calculations, or total numbers natively.</p>

<ul>
<li>To show formulas, insert them as images (Equatio, CodeCogs, or Office).</li>
<li>To calculate or total responses, export to Google Sheets and use spreadsheet formulas, or build the form in a tool with a calculation field.</li>
<li>To auto-grade, Google Forms quizzes grade multiple choice and dropdown answers, but short-answer math usually needs a manual check.</li>
</ul>

<p>The rest of this guide covers the formula side first, then the calculation and grading side near the end.</p>

<h2>Why Math Is Tricky in Google Forms</h2>

<p>Google Forms was built for plain text and images, not for math. Notation needs stacked fractions, roots, exponents, and operators that sit above or below the line, and a text field flattens all of it.</p>

<p>So "x squared over two" becomes "x^2/2", and a student has to guess what you meant. That single gap is why many teachers default back to paper. The workarounds below close it without leaving Google Forms.</p>

<h2>Method 1: Equatio Chrome Extension (Best for Frequent Use)</h2>

<p>Equatio is the go-to if you build math forms often. It adds an equation button straight into Google Forms, so you never leave the page.</p>

<ul>
<li>Install it. In the Chrome Web Store, search Equatio and click Add to Chrome. Sign in with the same Google account you use for Forms.</li>
<li>Pin it. Pin the Equatio icon to your toolbar so it is one click away.</li>
<li>Open your form. Create or open a quiz. A small blue Equatio icon (the pi symbol) appears next to question fields and answer options.</li>
<li>Build the equation. Click the icon to open the editor at the bottom of the screen. Type "fraction" and it drops in a fraction template. Prefer talking or writing? Use the speak-math or handwriting input.</li>
<li>Insert it. Click Insert Math and the equation lands in your form as a clean image.</li>
</ul>

<div class="note">
<p>Note: only you need Equatio. Students see the finished image and answer as normal, no install on their side.</p>
</div>

<h2>Method 2: CodeCogs Equation Editor (No Install)</h2>

<p>Only need an equation or two? CodeCogs is a free web tool, no extension required.</p>

<ul>
<li>Open the editor. Go to the CodeCogs equation editor in your browser.</li>
<li>Build the formula. Assemble it from the toolbar; it renders live above the buttons as you go.</li>
<li>Copy the image URL. Scroll to the small yellow box, choose URL from the dropdown, and copy the address.</li>
<li>Add it to your form. In Google Forms, click the image icon next to a question or answer, open the By URL tab, paste, preview, and click Add image.</li>
</ul>

<p>Good for occasional use. If you are inserting a dozen equations per quiz, Equatio will be faster.</p>

<h2>Method 3: Microsoft Word or PowerPoint</h2>

<p>Already comfortable in Office? Build the equation there and bring it across as an image.</p>

<ul>
<li>Create the equation. In Word or PowerPoint, go to Insert, then Equation, and type your formula with the symbol picker.</li>
<li>Capture it. Take a screenshot of the rendered equation.</li>
<li>Upload it. Back in Google Forms, click the image icon, open the Upload tab, and add the screenshot.</li>
</ul>

<p>Slowest of the three for volume, but handy when you already have equations sitting in Office files.</p>
