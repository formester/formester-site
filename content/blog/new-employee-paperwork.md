<p style="font-size: inherit;">New employee paperwork is the bundle of forms a hire signs on day 1 (or earlier). Done right, it takes 30 minutes and the new hire never thinks about it again. Done wrong, it eats day 1, frustrates the hire, and creates compliance gaps that come back during an audit. This guide gives you the complete checklist of forms US employers should have ready, what each one is for, who needs which, and how to handle the digital signing.</p>

<h2>The complete US new employee paperwork checklist</h2>

<p>The forms below are organized in the order most US employers collect them. The federal forms (I-9, W-4, direct deposit) are required for every hire. The state and company-specific forms vary.</p>

<h2>Federal forms (required for every US hire)</h2>

<h3>Form I-9 (Employment Eligibility Verification)</h3>

<p>Required by the US Citizenship and Immigration Services. Confirms the new hire is authorized to work in the US. Two sections: Section 1 (employee fills out by end of day 1) and Section 2 (employer verifies original identity and work-authorization documents within 3 business days of start).</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Penalty for missing:</strong> Civil penalties from $250 to $2,500+ per missing form (current as of 2026).</li>
<li><strong>Retention:</strong> Keep for 3 years after hire date or 1 year after termination, whichever is later.</li>
<li><strong>Common mistake:</strong> Filling Section 2 without seeing original documents. Photocopies do not count.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Form W-4 (Employee's Withholding Certificate)</h3>

<p>Tells the employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. Should be completed before the first payroll run.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Penalty for missing:</strong> Default to withholding at the highest single-filer rate.</li>
<li><strong>Retention:</strong> Keep for 4 years.</li>
<li><strong>Common mistake:</strong> Employees treating the W-4 as a one-time form. They can update it anytime, and should after major life events.</li>
</ul>

<h3>State W-4 equivalent</h3>

<p>Most US states require a state-level withholding form. Some accept the federal W-4 (e.g., CO, ND), most require their own (e.g., CA DE-4, NY IT-2104, IL W-4). Check your state's department of revenue.</p>

<h2>Onboarding and payroll setup</h2>

<h3>Direct deposit authorization</h3>

<p>Bank routing and account number for paycheck deposit. Includes a voided check or bank letter as verification. Not legally required (you can pay by physical check), but most US employees expect direct deposit.</p>

<h3>Emergency contact form</h3>

<p>Name, relationship, phone, and address of one to two emergency contacts. Not a federal requirement but standard practice for any workplace.</p>

<h3>Personal information form</h3>

<p>Home address, phone, marital status (for benefits enrollment), date of birth. Often combined with the emergency contact form.</p>

<h3>Tax forms for specific situations</h3>

<p>Form 8233 for non-resident alien employees claiming tax treaty benefits. Form W-9 for independent contractors (not full-time employees, but worth noting).</p>

<h2>Benefits enrollment</h2>

<p>If you offer benefits, the new hire signs up for each one. Standard package:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Health insurance enrollment.</strong> Plan selection, dependent enrollment, beneficiaries. Some plans have 30 to 60 day eligibility waits.</li>
<li><strong>Dental and vision enrollment.</strong> Usually optional add-ons.</li>
<li><strong>401(k) enrollment.</strong> Contribution percentage, investment allocation, beneficiary designation. Many companies enroll automatically and let employees opt out.</li>
<li><strong>HSA / FSA enrollment.</strong> Annual contribution election if applicable.</li>
<li><strong>Life insurance and disability.</strong> Basic coverage usually employer-paid; supplemental is opt-in.</li>
<li><strong>Commuter benefits, dependent care benefits, other pre-tax accounts.</strong> Where offered.</li>
</ul>

<p>Most employers route benefits through a single platform (Gusto, Rippling, ADP, BambooHR) so the new hire enrolls in everything via one workflow.</p>

<h2>Company policies (acknowledgement, not signature)</h2>

<p>These are not government forms but every employer should collect signed acknowledgements:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Employee handbook acknowledgement.</strong> The new hire confirms they received and read the handbook. Critical for at-will employment defense.</li>
<li><strong>Code of conduct acknowledgement.</strong> Specific to your company's policies.</li>
<li><strong>Confidentiality agreement.</strong> Standard for most roles.</li>
<li><strong>Non-disclosure agreement (NDA).</strong> Either as a standalone or built into the offer letter.</li>
<li><strong>Non-compete or non-solicit.</strong> Use only where enforceable (heavily restricted in CA, MA, several others as of 2026).</li>
<li><strong>Intellectual property assignment.</strong> Assigns work product to the company.</li>
<li><strong>Equipment policy.</strong> Acknowledgement of acceptable use for company laptop, phone, accounts.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Role-specific paperwork</h2>

<p>Some roles need additional forms.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Background check authorization.</strong> Separate, standalone document per FCRA rules. Use a structured <a href="/templates/background-check-authorization-form-22780/">background check form</a> so the disclosure and authorization are signed before screening starts.</li>
<li><strong>Drug screening consent.</strong> Required for DOT-regulated and safety-sensitive roles.</li>
<li><strong>License or certification confirmation.</strong> Healthcare, legal, and other licensed professions.</li>
<li><strong>Driver's license + MVR consent.</strong> For roles involving driving.</li>
<li><strong>Garnishment forms.</strong> If applicable (court-ordered wage withholding).</li>
</ul>

<h2>Pre-day-1 vs day-1 paperwork</h2>

<p>The smartest move is to push as much paperwork to BEFORE day 1 as the law allows. Three things to watch:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>I-9 Section 1</strong> must be completed by end of day 1, not earlier. The employee cannot complete it before they start. Section 2 (employer review) can happen days 1 to 3.</li>
<li><strong>W-4 and state withholding</strong> can be completed any time before the first payroll run. Push to pre-day-1.</li>
<li><strong>Benefits enrollment</strong> often has an eligibility wait, so plan around that.</li>
</ul>

<p>The companies that do onboarding well send the new hire a pre-boarding packet 1 to 2 weeks before start. Direct deposit, W-4, state withholding, emergency contact, handbook acknowledgement, and benefits enrollment can all be done before day 1. Day 1 is just the I-9 Section 1 + 2 verification and any role-specific forms.</p>

<p>If you do not already have a structured pre-boarding flow, see our companion guide on the <a href="/blog/employee-onboarding-process/">employee onboarding process</a> for the 7-stage framework.</p>

<h2>Going digital: e-signature and storage</h2>

<p>Three principles for moving paperwork off paper:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>E-signatures are legal for almost everything.</strong> The federal E-SIGN Act and state UETA laws make e-signatures valid for almost all employment paperwork. The big exception is certain immigration forms that have specific signature requirements.</li>
<li><strong>Store securely.</strong> Personal data (SSN, DOB, addresses, bank info) needs encryption at rest and access controls. Cloud HR platforms (BambooHR, Gusto, Rippling) handle this; spreadsheets and shared drives do not.</li>
<li><strong>Set retention rules.</strong> I-9: 3 years post-hire or 1 year post-termination, whichever is later. W-4: 4 years. Personnel records: varies by state (most require at least 3 years post-termination). Build the retention into your platform's automated deletion.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Common mistakes that show up in audits</h2>

<p>The patterns that come up most often when an I-9 or DOL audit happens:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Missing I-9 forms.</strong> The most common finding. Either never collected or stored where nobody can find them. $250 to $2,500 per missing form.</li>
<li><strong>I-9 Section 2 incomplete or late.</strong> Section 2 must be completed within 3 business days. "We do it later when we have time" is the most expensive procrastination in HR.</li>
<li><strong>Photocopying I-9 documents for some hires but not others.</strong> Inconsistent practice triggers discrimination findings. Either photocopy for everyone or no one.</li>
<li><strong>Background check authorization buried in the offer letter.</strong> FCRA requires standalone disclosure. The 2018 9th Circuit ruling (Gilberg v. California Check Cashing Stores) made this a high-cost mistake.</li>
<li><strong>State-specific forms not collected.</strong> California, New York, and Massachusetts have additional employee-information requirements. Easy to miss if your HR platform is configured for federal-only.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The takeaway</h2>

<p>New employee paperwork is a checklist problem, not a hard problem. The federal forms (I-9, W-4) are non-negotiable and have real penalties for missing. The state forms vary. The benefits and policy forms are company-specific but standardized within your company. Push as much to pre-day-1 as the law allows, use e-signature for everything except where prohibited, and audit your stack once a year for the state and role-specific additions. Done right, the new hire signs everything in one session and the rest of onboarding focuses on actually getting them productive.</p>
