<p style="font-size: inherit;">Hiring manager interview questions are different from generic interview questions in one specific way: they are asked by the person the candidate will report to, in the round that decides the hire. The bar is higher. The questions need to surface real fit, real skill, and real motivation, not just rehearsed answers. The 30 questions below are the ones experienced hiring managers actually ask, grouped by what each one is meant to surface.</p>

<p>Use them as a question bank. Pick 6 to 10 per interview based on the role and the specific gaps you noticed in earlier rounds. Asking all 30 is too many and runs the candidate dry.</p>

<h2>What hiring manager interview questions are for</h2>

<p>The hiring manager interview happens after the recruiter screen and (usually) at least one peer or technical round. By the time the candidate gets to the hiring manager, you already know they meet the bar on paper. The hiring manager round answers three questions the earlier rounds cannot:</p>

<ul>
<li>Will this person actually work the way I need them to?</li>
<li>Is this role the right fit for them, and will they stay?</li>
<li>Do I want them on my team day to day?</li>
</ul>

<p>The questions below are designed for those three goals, not for verifying skills (your peer/technical round does that).</p>

<h2>Open the interview: motivation and context</h2>

<p>Start with broad questions that let the candidate set the scene. These also surface how they think about their career.</p>

<ol>
<li>Walk me through your career so far. What were the inflection points?</li>
<li>What is drawing you to this specific role?</li>
<li>What are you looking for in your next role that you do not have in your current one?</li>
<li>What about our company specifically interested you enough to apply?</li>
<li>Where do you want to be in 3 years, and how does this role fit?</li>
</ol>

<h2>Role-specific fit</h2>

<p>These are tailored to the role you are hiring for. The pattern: describe a real situation the role faces, then ask how the candidate would think about it.</p>

<ol start="6">
<li>If I gave you 90 days to make an impact in this role, what would you focus on first?</li>
<li>Tell me about a project from your past that is closest to what this role does.</li>
<li>What is the part of this role you would find hardest?</li>
<li>What do you think would be your biggest contribution here in the first year?</li>
<li>What are 2 to 3 things you would need from me as a manager to do this role well?</li>
</ol>

<h2>How they actually work</h2>

<p>These surface working style, communication, and how they handle the day-to-day. Pair these with structured <a href="/blog/behavioral-interview-questions/">behavioral interview questions</a> from earlier rounds so you see both the abstract pattern and the concrete story.</p>

<ol start="11">
<li>Describe the working environment where you do your best work.</li>
<li>How do you prefer to receive feedback?</li>
<li>How do you decide what to work on each week?</li>
<li>What does a good 1:1 with your manager look like to you?</li>
<li>How do you handle stakeholders who keep changing their mind?</li>
</ol>

<h2>Decision-making and judgement</h2>

<p>How the candidate thinks through hard calls is one of the highest-signal areas you can probe. Most candidates have answers prepared for the soft questions; the judgement questions force them to reason in real time.</p>

<ol start="16">
<li>Walk me through a decision you made recently that you are still unsure about.</li>
<li>Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager. What happened?</li>
<li>What is a piece of conventional wisdom in your field you think is wrong?</li>
<li>Describe a project where you had to push back on a stakeholder. How did you handle it?</li>
<li>How do you decide when to escalate versus when to push through on your own?</li>
</ol>

<h2>Failure and self-awareness</h2>

<p>The most diagnostic block in any interview. Strong candidates have a real, specific failure they can describe with a real, specific lesson. Weak candidates either claim no failures or give humble-brag failures ("I work too hard").</p>

<ol start="21">
<li>Tell me about your biggest professional failure. What did you learn?</li>
<li>What feedback have you received most often that you are still working on?</li>
<li>If I called your last manager, what would they say is your biggest growth area?</li>
<li>What is a skill you wish you were better at, and how are you working on it?</li>
<li>Describe a time you got something wrong and had to walk it back publicly.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Motivation and longevity</h2>

<p>The questions that surface whether the candidate will still be in this role 18 months from now.</p>

<ol start="26">
<li>What would have to be true 12 months from now for you to feel like this role was the right choice?</li>
<li>When was the last time you got really excited about something at work?</li>
<li>What do you do when you are bored or unmotivated at work?</li>
<li>If we offered you a counter-offer-quality raise to stay where you are right now, would you take it? Why or why not?</li>
<li>If you got an offer from us and a more senior role at a competitor for less money, how would you decide?</li>
</ol>

<h2>How to score the answers</h2>

<p>Three things make hiring-manager-round scoring more useful than a gut check:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Pre-define what a great answer looks like for each question.</strong> Before the interview, write 1 sentence on what a strong answer would cover. Read the candidate's answer against that, not against your overall impression.</li>
<li><strong>Score per question, not overall.</strong> A 1 to 4 score per question (1 = unable to answer, 4 = strong concrete answer with insight). Add up at the end. This removes the "I liked them" halo from the final score.</li>
<li><strong>Note the things they did not say.</strong> A candidate who never mentions teammates in their stories may be more individualistic than the role allows. A candidate who never names a metric may be allergic to measurement. The absence of certain themes is data.</li>
</ol>

<p>Pair the question list with a structured <a href="/templates/reference-forms-template/">interview scorecard</a> so every interviewer scores the same dimensions and you can compare candidates apples-to-apples.</p>

<h2>What to do after the interview</h2>

<p>Three moves that turn the hiring manager round into a hiring signal:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Score within 30 minutes.</strong> Memory degrades fast. Write your notes and final score before you do anything else.</li>
<li><strong>Compare with the panel.</strong> If your read disagrees with the technical or peer round, dig into why before defaulting to your own view. Sometimes the hiring manager catches what others missed; sometimes the panel saw a side the candidate hid.</li>
<li><strong>Reference check the gaps.</strong> If the interview surfaced a possible concern (judgement, follow-through, conflict style), build a reference check question specifically targeting that concern, not generic ones.</li>
</ol>

<h2>The takeaway</h2>

<p>The hiring manager round decides the hire. The questions you ask here have to surface the three things the earlier rounds cannot: working style, role fit, and motivation. Pick 6 to 10 from the lists above, pre-define what good answers look like, score per-question rather than overall, and compare against the panel before making the call. Done well, this round closes the loop on whether the candidate is the right hire. Done badly, it is a 60-minute chat that confirms what you already thought.</p>
