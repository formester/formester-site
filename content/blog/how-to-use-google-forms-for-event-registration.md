<p style="font-size: inherit;">Google Forms handles event registration well for most events. You can have a form collecting names in about ten minutes, the answers arrive in a spreadsheet you can work from on the day, and Google Forms will now close the form itself when you hit your seat number or your deadline. That last part is new, and it removes the workaround every older guide on this subject still recommends. What is left is the money: there is no way to take a ticket payment on the form.</p>

<div class="tldr">
<p><strong>Quick answer</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>You need a form today:</strong> blank form, name it after the event and the date, then ask for name, email, ticket or session choice, plus anything you have to know in advance. About ten minutes of work.</li>
<li><strong>Your event has a capacity:</strong> open the Published options dialog and use Set close date or response limit. Google Forms closes registration at your number or your date without an add-on.</li>
<li><strong>Your event is paid:</strong> there is still no payment question. Send people to a checkout link after they register, or build the form somewhere that takes payment on submit.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 id="build">How do you make a Google Form for event registration?</h2>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/5cd5480b2d1f28bf_gforms-event-registration-build.gif" alt="Building an event registration form in Google Forms with name, email and a ticket type question, each marked required" loading="lazy"></p>

<p>Six steps, and none of them need a paid plan or an add-on.</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li>
<h3>Start a blank form and name it after the event</h3>
<p>Open <a href="https://forms.google.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener">forms.google.com</a> and click <strong>Blank form</strong>. Name it with the event and the date, for example "Autumn Product Workshop, 14 October". That name is what people see at the top of the form and what you will search Drive for in six months.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Put the practical details in the description</h3>
<p>The line under the title is the description. Put the date, the start time, the address or joining link and the registration deadline here. Organisers who skip this field answer the same three questions by email all week.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Ask who they are</h3>
<p>Two <strong>Short answer</strong> questions: full name and email address. Mark both <strong>Required</strong>. If you need to reach people on the day, add a phone number. Leave it optional unless you genuinely will call.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Ask which one they are coming to</h3>
<p>If there is more than one session, ticket type or time slot, add a <strong>Multiple choice</strong> question listing them. This one question is what turns a list of names into something you can plan room sizes and catering from.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Set the response rules</h3>
<p>Open <strong>Settings</strong> and expand <strong>Responses</strong>. Set <strong>Collect email addresses</strong> to Verified, which records the signed-in address, or to Responder input if you would rather people typed one. Turning on <strong>Send responders a copy of their response</strong> emails every registrant what they submitted, which is the closest thing to a confirmation. <strong>Limit to 1 response</strong> stops the same person registering twice, with one catch: it sits under a REQUIRES SIGN IN heading, so it loses anyone without a Google account.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Preview it on a phone, then publish</h3>
<p>Click the eye icon and open the form on your own phone. Most registrations come from a phone, usually from a link in a message. Then click <strong>Publish</strong>, set the responders to <strong>Anyone with the link</strong>, and take the link from the <strong>Copy responder link</strong> panel that opens.</p>
</li>
</ol>

<h2 id="questions">Which questions should an event registration form ask?</h2>

<p>Keep it to what you will actually use. Every extra question costs you registrations, and a form asking fourteen things for a free webinar gets abandoned. Here is a list to copy, grouped by why you are asking.</p>

<div class="table-wrap">
<table>
<thead><tr><th>Question</th><th>Type</th><th>Required</th><th>Why you need it</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Full name</td><td>Short answer</td><td>Yes</td><td>The badge, the list at the door</td></tr>
<tr><td>Email address</td><td>Short answer</td><td>Yes</td><td>Confirmation, reminders, the recording afterwards</td></tr>
<tr><td>Organisation or job title</td><td>Short answer</td><td>No</td><td>Only if the audience mix changes how you run the day</td></tr>
<tr><td>Which session are you attending?</td><td>Multiple choice</td><td>Yes</td><td>Room size, catering numbers, materials</td></tr>
<tr><td>Ticket type</td><td>Multiple choice</td><td>Yes</td><td>Standard, student or member pricing</td></tr>
<tr><td>Dietary requirements</td><td>Checkboxes plus Other</td><td>No</td><td>Only when you are serving food</td></tr>
<tr><td>Accessibility requirements</td><td>Paragraph</td><td>No</td><td>Ask on every in-person event, and read the answers</td></tr>
<tr><td>How did you hear about this?</td><td>Multiple choice</td><td>No</td><td>The one marketing question worth its cost</td></tr>
<tr><td>Photography consent</td><td>Multiple choice, yes or no</td><td>Yes</td><td>If you will publish photos of the room</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>Two questions that are usually a mistake: a mailing address, unless you are posting something, and a free-text "anything else we should know" box, which produces a hundred answers you will not read. If you want one open question, make it the accessibility one.</p>

<h2 id="limit">How do you stop registrations when the event is full?</h2>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/846044d3c5b40cb2_gforms-response-limit.gif" alt="Setting a response limit of 50 in the Google Forms Stop accepting responses dialog so registration closes itself" loading="lazy"></p>

<p>Google Forms will do this for you now. Click the slider icon on the <strong>Published</strong> button to open <strong>Published options</strong>, then click <strong>Set close date or response limit</strong>. The dialog gives you two choices:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>On a date:</strong> pick a date and time, and the form stops accepting registrations at that moment. This is your registration deadline, handled without you being at a laptop.</li>
<li><strong>After a number of responses:</strong> type your seat count. Once the form reaches it, registration closes. Fifty seats means typing 50 and forgetting about it.</li>
</ul>

<p>The same dialog holds the message people see once the form has closed. It reads "This form is no longer accepting responses" by default, and the pencil icon lets you replace it with something more useful, such as a waitlist address. Back in Published options, the confirmation line then reads "Responses accepted until 50 responses have been received", so you can check the cap took.</p>

<p>Two things worth knowing. You can still close registration by hand at any point by switching <strong>Accepting responses</strong> off in that dialog. And closing a form is not the same as unpublishing it: a closed form shows your message, while an unpublished form shows a blank page, which looks broken to anyone holding your link.</p>

<p>If you have read elsewhere that Google Forms needs an add-on for this, that was true until recently. Our own write-up of <a href="/blog/form-limiter-add-on-for-google-forms/">the Form Limiter add-on</a> covers that route, and you no longer need it for a straightforward seat cap.</p>

<h2 id="payment">How do you collect payment for a paid event?</h2>

<p>This one is still a real gap. Google Forms cannot take a payment, because there is no payment question type, so the money has to happen somewhere else. Three routes, in order of how much work they are:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>A checkout link in the confirmation message:</strong> take the registration in Google Forms, then send people to a Stripe payment link or a PayPal.me address on the screen that appears after they submit. Free, and nothing ties a payment back to a registration, so you reconcile the two lists by hand.</li>
<li><strong>An add-on:</strong> a Workspace add-on such as Payable Forms puts a checkout inside the form and charges a fee per transaction on top of your processor's. Worth it when the volume makes manual reconciliation painful.</li>
<li><strong>A form builder that takes payment:</strong> the payment sits on the form, so the registration and the payment are one record. This is the only route where your attendee list is also your paid list.</li>
</ul>

<p>The full comparison, with the real fees, is in <a href="/blog/how-to-add-payment-option-in-google-forms/">adding payment to a Google Form</a>.</p>

<h2 id="share">How do you share the form and see who registered?</h2>

<p>Publish the form, then take the link from <strong>Copy responder link</strong>, with <strong>Shorten URL</strong> ticked if it is going on anything printed. For posters and table cards you want a QR code, which Google Forms does not generate, so that comes from a QR tool or from a builder that has one built in.</p>

<p>On the registration list itself, the <strong>Responses</strong> tab gives you a live count and a summary of every answer. Click <strong>Link to Sheets</strong> and choose <strong>Create a new spreadsheet</strong> to get the attendee list you will actually work from, which you can sort and filter, and share with the person on the door without giving them access to the form. <a href="/blog/how-to-link-google-sheets-to-google-forms/">Linking a Google Form to Sheets</a> covers the connection and what breaks it.</p>

<p>One habit worth keeping: share the spreadsheet with your team, not the form. Anyone with editor access to the form can change the questions mid-registration, which is how a required field disappears halfway through a campaign.</p>

<h2 id="cannot">What Google Forms cannot do for an event</h2>

<p>Straight list, so you know what you are taking on before the registrations start:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>No payments:</strong> no payment field, so paid tickets need a second system.</li>
<li><strong>No ticket or reference number:</strong> registrants can get an email copy of their answers, but there is no booking code and nothing to show at the door.</li>
<li><strong>No check-in:</strong> nothing to scan on the day and no attendance record beyond your own spreadsheet.</li>
<li><strong>No waitlist:</strong> when the form closes, the next person reads your closing message rather than joining a queue.</li>
<li><strong>No reminder emails:</strong> Google Forms sends nothing as the date approaches. Reminders are yours to send.</li>
<li><strong>No QR code:</strong> fine for a link in a message, a problem for a poster or a table card.</li>
<li><strong>Little branding:</strong> a header image and a colour, with the Google footer on every form.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a free internal session, none of that matters. For a paid conference, most of it does.</p>
