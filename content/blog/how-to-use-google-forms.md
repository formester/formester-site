<p style="font-size: inherit;">Google Forms is a free form builder that comes with any Google account. You build a form in the browser, publish it, then share a link. Every answer lands in one place you can read or push into a spreadsheet. Four steps cover the whole thing: make the form, publish and share it, read the answers, then work out where it stops.</p>

<div class="tldr">
<p><strong>Quick answer</strong></p>
<ul>
<li><strong>You want a form in the next five minutes:</strong> go to forms.google.com, click Blank form, name it, then add your questions. There is nothing to install and nothing to pay.</li>
<li><strong>You want people to fill it in:</strong> click Publish, choose who can respond, then use Copy responder link. The old Send button is gone, which is the change most guides have not caught up with.</li>
<li><strong>You want the answers in a spreadsheet:</strong> open the Responses tab and click Link to Sheets. Every new answer appears as a row from then on.</li>
</ul>
</div>

<h2 id="what">What is Google Forms?</h2>

<p>Google Forms is part of Google Workspace, alongside Docs and Sheets. It is free with a personal Google account, and it is included in the paid Workspace plans that businesses and schools use. You need an account to build a form. The people answering it usually do not.</p>

<p>What you get is a single-column form with a title, a description and as many questions as you want. Google stores the answers for you, charts the multiple-choice ones automatically, and will push everything into a Google Sheet if you ask it to. There is no response limit on the free plan and no cap on how many forms you make.</p>

<p>What it is used for, in practice:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Collecting information from a group:</strong> event registrations, class sign-ups, order requests, job applications.</li>
<li><strong>Asking people what they think:</strong> surveys, feedback after a session, satisfaction scores.</li>
<li><strong>Testing what people know:</strong> quizzes with automatic marking, which is a setting rather than a separate product.</li>
<li><strong>Getting files:</strong> a File upload question, though it makes the person answering sign in to a Google account first.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="make">How do you make a Google Form?</h2>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/737e967150f7f44f_gforms-build-question.gif" alt="Marking a question required in Google Forms, then adding a multiple choice question with two options" loading="lazy"></p>

<p>Six steps take you from an empty page to a form that is ready to publish. Nothing here needs a paid plan.</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li>
<h3>Open a blank form</h3>
<p>Go to <a href="https://forms.google.com" target="_blank" rel="noopener">forms.google.com</a> and click <strong>Blank form</strong>. The template gallery sits on the same screen if you would rather start from one of Google's, but the blank form is faster to understand.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Name the form</h3>
<p>Click <strong>Untitled form</strong> in the coloured header and type a real name. That name is the title people see at the top of the form. The file name in the very top left is a separate field, so change that too if you want to find this form in Drive later. The line under the title is a description, which is where instructions and a deadline go.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Add your first question</h3>
<p>Click the question text and type the question. Then open the dropdown on the right to pick the type. <strong>Short answer</strong> suits a name, <strong>Multiple choice</strong> suits one pick from a list, <strong>Checkboxes</strong> suits several picks, and <strong>Dropdown</strong> keeps a long list tidy. Google often guesses the type from your wording, so check it rather than assume it.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Add the rest, in the order people will answer them</h3>
<p>Use the <strong>+</strong> button in the floating toolbar for each new question. Google Forms shows one column of questions in the order you place them, so drag them with the handle at the top of each card until the sequence reads properly.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Mark the questions you actually need</h3>
<p>Turn on <strong>Required</strong> at the bottom right of a question card and nobody can submit without answering it. Use it sparingly. Every required question is one more reason for somebody to abandon the form.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Check it as a respondent</h3>
<p>Click the eye icon to open <strong>Preview</strong>. This shows what people will see, which is the only reliable way to catch a question that made sense to you and to nobody else. Most people will open your form on a phone, so look at it there too.</p>
</li>
</ol>

<p>Two shortcuts worth knowing. The copy icon on a question card duplicates that question with its options intact, which saves retyping a five-option list. And the three-dot menu at the top right has <strong>Make a copy</strong>, so next month's version starts from this month's rather than from nothing.</p>

<h2 id="send">How do you publish and share a Google Form?</h2>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/d171fe7b52a633db_gforms-publish-responder-link.gif" alt="Publishing a Google Form, choosing who can respond, then copying the responder link with Shorten URL ticked" loading="lazy"></p>

<p>This is the part that changed. There is no <strong>Send</strong> button any more. A form has to be published before anyone can answer it, and the sharing options moved with it.</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li>
<h3>Publish the form</h3>
<p>Click <strong>Publish</strong> in the top right. The dialog asks who the responders are: <strong>Anyone with the link</strong>, or restricted to your organisation on a Workspace account. Click <strong>Publish</strong> and the button changes to <strong>Published</strong>. Until you do this, anyone opening the link sees a blank page.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Copy the responder link</h3>
<p>Publishing opens a <strong>Copy responder link</strong> panel straight away, and the chain icon in the toolbar reopens it any time. Tick <strong>Shorten URL</strong> for a forms.gle address that fits in a message or on a poster, then click <strong>Copy</strong>.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Or send it to specific people</h3>
<p>The person icon opens <strong>Share</strong>, the same sheet you know from Drive, where you can add people, groups or calendar events. Note the two settings underneath: <strong>Editor view</strong> controls who can change the form and <strong>Responder view</strong> controls who can answer it. Mixing those up is how a colleague ends up editing a live form.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Or embed it in your own page</h3>
<p>The three-dot menu holds <strong>Embed HTML</strong>, which gives you an iframe with width and height fields. The default height is usually too short and cuts the form off, so raise it. Our guide to <a href="/blog/how-to-embed-a-google-form/">embedding a Google Form</a> covers what breaks and how to fix it.</p>
</li>
</ol>

<p>Before you share anything, open <strong>Settings</strong> and expand <strong>Responses</strong>. Three settings there decide how the form behaves. <strong>Collect email addresses</strong> is a dropdown, where Verified records the signed-in address and Responder input asks people to type one. <strong>Send responders a copy of their response</strong> emails them what they submitted, and needs the first setting on. <strong>Limit to 1 response</strong> sits under a heading that reads REQUIRES SIGN IN, because it does exactly that and loses anyone without a Google account.</p>

<h2 id="responses">How do you see the answers?</h2>

<p><img src="https://formester-strapi.s3.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com/c0873809b92b4ab0_gforms-responses-link-to-sheets.gif" alt="The Google Forms Responses tab with its summary charts, then linking the form to a new Google Sheet" loading="lazy"></p>

<p>Open the <strong>Responses</strong> tab at the top of the editor. The number beside it is your live total, and three views split the same data three ways.</p>

<ol class="steps">
<li>
<h3>Read the summary</h3>
<p><strong>Summary</strong> charts every question for you. Multiple choice and checkboxes become bar or pie charts. Scales become histograms, and short answers become a list. For anything beyond that, see <a href="/blog/how-to-make-pie-charts-in-google-forms/">making pie charts from Google Forms responses</a>.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Look at one question, or one person, at a time</h3>
<p><strong>Question</strong> steps through the form question by question, which is how you find the one field everybody misread. <strong>Individual</strong> shows one submission per screen, which is where you go when somebody says they registered and you cannot find them.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Send it to a spreadsheet</h3>
<p>Click <strong>Link to Sheets</strong>, then <strong>Create a new spreadsheet</strong>. Every answer already collected moves across, and every new one arrives as a row within seconds. <a href="/blog/how-to-link-google-sheets-to-google-forms/">Linking Google Forms to Sheets</a> covers what breaks that connection.</p>
</li>
<li>
<h3>Close the form when you are done</h3>
<p>Click the slider icon on the <strong>Published</strong> button and switch <strong>Accepting responses</strong> off. In the same dialog, <strong>Set close date or response limit</strong> closes the form on a date and time you pick, or once it reaches a number of responses you set, and the message people see when it closes is editable.</p>
</li>
</ol>

<p>The three-dot menu inside the Responses tab holds the rest: email notifications for new responses, plus a CSV download and printing. There are <a href="/blog/5-ways-to-view-responses-in-google-forms/">five different ways to view Google Forms responses</a> depending on what you are trying to find.</p>

<h2 id="limits">What can Google Forms not do?</h2>

<p>The free price is real and so are the gaps. These are the ones people hit first, in roughly the order they hit them.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>It cannot take a payment:</strong> there is no payment question type. Paid registrations need a checkout link, an add-on, or a builder with payments built in. See <a href="/blog/how-to-add-payment-option-in-google-forms/">adding payment to a Google Form</a>.</li>
<li><strong>It cannot route an approval:</strong> there is no approve or reject step, so a request form is a list of requests and nothing more.</li>
<li><strong>It cannot carry your brand:</strong> there is no logo slot, no custom font beyond Google's list and no CSS. The Google footer stays on every form.</li>
<li><strong>It cannot collect a signature:</strong> there is no signature field, only an image upload as a workaround. See <a href="/blog/add-signature-in-google-forms/">adding a signature to a Google Form</a>.</li>
<li><strong>It cannot calculate anything:</strong> no totals, no price maths, no scoring outside quiz mode.</li>
<li><strong>File upload forces a sign-in:</strong> the moment a form asks for a file, the person answering must have a Google account and be logged in, which quietly loses anyone who does not.</li>
</ul>

<p>One old complaint is worth crossing off. Google Forms used to have no way to cap responses or close on a date, and most guides on the subject still send you to an add-on for it. It is now a built-in setting, described in the last step above.</p>
